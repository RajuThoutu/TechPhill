AI coding tools make a reasonable question harder to ignore: if we can generate an application ourselves, why should we keep buying enterprise platforms?

I think it is a useful challenge. Platforms should have to justify their place. But before answering it, I would separate the ability to produce software from the responsibility of running a business capability.

A working application is part of that capability. So are the rules it follows, the people allowed to use it, the systems it depends on, and the evidence it leaves behind. Those responsibilities remain, whichever tools produce the code.

## What are we actually buying

Consider a customer service process. A demonstration might show a request arriving, an AI assistant understanding it, and a response appearing on screen. That is a promising start.

The operational questions arrive quickly. Who owns the request when it cannot be resolved? Which actions need approval? Can a customer service representative see every field? What happens when a downstream system times out after accepting an update? How does someone establish what happened six months later?

Then the business changes its policy. Cases already in progress may need different treatment from new cases. A team may need to update a rule without accidentally changing a related process. Someone has to test the change and decide whether it is safe to release.

These are ordinary requirements of running a business process. They can be implemented in custom software, assembled from services, or supported by a platform. The choice changes where the work lives and who takes responsibility for it.

That is why I would evaluate an enterprise platform as a collection of business capabilities, rather than simply as a way to avoid writing code.

## A fair comparison includes the work after launch

AI makes custom development worth reconsidering. A focused application with clear boundaries may be a better fit than a large platform. An engineering team may also want more control over its architecture, interfaces, or release decisions.

I would take that option seriously. I would also ask the team to describe the complete operating model.

Who maintains access controls as roles change? Who owns integration failures, monitoring, recovery, and support? How are business rules versioned? Can the organisation explain a decision and reconstruct the information available at the time? What happens when the people who built the system move on?

Buying a platform does not make those questions disappear. The customer still has to configure it well, govern its use, and understand the division of responsibility with the supplier. Poor implementation can undermine a capable product.

Building an alternative means accepting responsibility for whatever the chosen components do not provide. That may be entirely sensible. It should be an explicit decision, with people and processes behind it, rather than an assumption that faster code generation will cover everything later.

The comparison I want is between two credible ways of operating the same capability. A prototype on one side and a fully supported production service on the other would tell us very little.

## AI has two different jobs here

I find it helpful to separate two conversations that often get mixed together.

The first is AI helping teams build and change software. That can include drafting code, suggesting tests, explaining an existing implementation, or helping translate a requirement into a design. These tools can be used in custom development and, where supported, within platform development. Their output still needs appropriate review and validation.

The second is AI helping the business perform work at runtime. Think of interpreting an incoming request, extracting information from a document, suggesting a next action, or helping a person make a decision.

For the second job, the surrounding process matters enormously. What may the AI act on? Which information is it allowed to access? When must a person approve an action? How is an uncertain result handled? Can an incorrect action be identified and corrected?

Generating the application more quickly does not answer those questions. Nor does adding an AI feature establish that the business process has improved. I would look for evidence in the work itself: whether requests are resolved appropriately, exceptions are handled, and people can understand and control the outcome.

## Platforms still have to earn their place

None of this gives established platforms a free pass. A platform may introduce constraints, complexity, or dependencies that outweigh the capabilities it provides. Features that looked valuable during selection may turn out to be difficult to change or barely used.

I would ask a supplier to show how a realistic process behaves when things become awkward. Change a rule. Remove a permission. Interrupt an integration. Introduce an exception. Trace a completed case. Then show how a team would maintain those behaviours over time.

I would ask the same of a custom solution. The standard should be consistent, even when the implementation is different.

For me, AI coding shifts the build-versus-buy discussion toward a better question: which approach gives this organisation the capability it needs, with an operating responsibility it can sustain?

Sometimes the answer will be an enterprise platform. Sometimes it will be custom software, or a combination of both. Faster development makes more options worth examining. The choice still depends on what the business needs to run, change, and remain accountable for.
